## Introduction

If you use AI to write code, you have probably used the same model through more than one tool. Say,
Anthropic models in Claude Code vs Cursor, Pi, or even Cline. You may have noticed that the model's
approach and capabilities are different between tools.

That difference is measurable, and it is now something the models get trained against. The tool
wrapped around the model is called an **agent harness**. It runs the loop, decides which tools the
model can use, writes the context the model reads, interprets what comes back, and decides when to
stop. And the open model you run behind them was almost certainly trained inside exactly one of
them.

<Accordion title="What exactly counts as a harness?">

The harness is the program that runs the agent: the loop, the tool surface, the prompt and context
management, the retries, the stop condition. The **model** is only the next-token distribution
inside it, stateless between calls. The **sandbox** is the isolated place its actions actually run.
An **RL environment** is the thing on the other side of the loop, receiving actions and returning
observations. Hugging Face's [agent glossary](https://huggingface.co/blog/agent-glossary) has the
longer version. [The next chapter](#what-is-a-harness) works through where the boundaries sit and
why the harness/environment one decides how you train.

</Accordion>

### Model cards name the harness now

GLM-4.7 promises "significant improvements on complex tasks in mainstream agent frameworks such as
Claude Code, Kilo Code, Cline, and Roo Code" [@zai2025glm47]. Kimi K2 reports Terminal-Bench twice,
25.0 under Terminus and 30.0 inside Moonshot's own framework, as two rows of one table
[@kimi2025k2]. MiniMax M2 names a different harness for almost every benchmark it lists
[@minimax2025m2card]. DeepSeek-V3.2 checks its scores across Claude Code and RooCode, then admits
its thinking mode is incompatible with Terminus, so that number had to come from somewhere else
[@deepseek2025v32]. DeepSeek shipped a model whose thinking mode could not run under Terminus,
noticed, and reported the Terminal-Bench number from a different harness.

Which harness a model is good in has become a shipping claim, printed beside the benchmark numbers,
because almost nobody runs the weights on their own.

It gets worse than uneven scores. Run the same benchmark under two harnesses and the ranking between
models can flip. ByteDance's Seed-OSS card has two SWE-Bench Verified rows covering the same models,
and the order reverses twice between them [@bytedance2025seedoss]. Some of that gap is extra
sampling rather than the harness, and the reversals survive that objection anyway: more sampling can
raise a score, it cannot explain why one model passes another while a second model moves the
opposite way.

So "model A is better than model B" is not a harness-independent statement.

Anthropic is the clearest case of a lab that knows this, because it owns both ends. It ships Claude
and it ships Claude Code, and it trains one to use the other. The OpenEnv team put the mechanism
plainly: agent harnesses keep getting better partly because "models like GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.8 are
trained to use their respective harnesses" [@openenv2026]. Anthropic also said the quiet part early.
Its own 2024 SWE-bench write-up noted that "the performance of an agent on SWE-bench can vary
significantly based on this scaffolding, even when using the same underlying AI model"
[@anthropic2024swebench], and then went on building the scaffolding it measures against.

That is the asymmetry this article is about. When you own the model and the harness, the variance is
something you tune away. When all you have is the weights, it is something that happens to you.

### How badly it breaks

Not gently. The Orchard team took two open systems that had each been trained inside a single
harness and ran them in three, one of which neither system had seen in training
[@peng2026orchard].

The two halves of that figure say different things. Moving OpenSWE-32B off OpenHands, the harness it
was trained in, onto mini-swe-agent costs 7.5 points: a real tax, not a catastrophe, and the kind of
number you could talk yourself into ignoring. Moving it onto Kimi-CLI, which it had never seen,
costs 58.8 and leaves 3.6 percent of SWE-bench Verified. On Terminal-Bench 2.0 in that same unseen
harness it scores zero. Scale-SWE does not degrade at all, it simply stops producing valid tool
calls anywhere but at home. Orchard, trained across harnesses, stays inside a 19-point band across
all three and keeps 20.1 on the benchmark the other two systems cannot score on at all.

Those are the two failure modes, and Orchard names them: a **degraded resolve rate**, where the
model still behaves but solves less, and a **catastrophic format failure**, where the output stops
being parseable at all. The second one is the one you meet on a laptop. If you have ever watched a
local model call a tool its harness has never heard of, or spill raw markup into a field that wanted
JSON, that is a format failure at small scale. The model is not confused about the task. It is
confused about the interface, and the interface is the only thing it can see.

The KwaiKAT team names the failure, harness scaling, and describes it better than anyone
[@kwaikat2026katcoder]:

> "In agentic RL, if training relies solely on a single fixed harness, the model often learns not
> 'how to solve the task' but 'how to solve the task under that particular harness's interface
> conventions.'"

It shows up three ways, worth keeping for later. **Format overfitting** anchors the model to one
action format, so parsing breaks when the protocol changes. **Context-structure overfitting** ties
it to one harness's way of stacking up history. **Control-flow overfitting** leaves it leaning on
someone else's retry timing and stop conditions, unable to plan for itself once they are gone.

### Frontier labs now train across harnesses on purpose

The fix is to vary the harness during training, which KwaiKAT frames as domain randomization applied
to the environment. Two claims sit inside that, and they are easier to believe separately. The first
is that labs deliberately buy harness variety. The second is that buying it costs them nothing.

For the first: poolside's Laguna report has a section titled Multi-harness Training, and its opening
line is this article's thesis in someone else's words [@poolside2026laguna]:

> "To encourage generalization across diverse agent harnesses, our training data includes
> trajectories from external frameworks such as OpenHands, OpenCode, and Mini-SWE-Agent."

That is 1.3 billion tokens of trajectories from three harnesses that are not poolside's, with each
one's native behaviour deliberately preserved, down to its subagent spawning, context compaction,
planning scaffolds and reminder systems. poolside has a harness of its own, `pool`, the terminal
coding agent they ship and run every evaluation in, and their RL phase runs through `pool` itself,
so "any change to the deployed harness is also a change the policy is trained against." Supervised
training on other people's harnesses, reinforcement learning inside their own.

For the second: the worry is that spreading training across harnesses trades peak performance for
robustness. The one controlled ablation published so far says otherwise. OpenForgeRL trained the
same model on one harness and on three, and the three-harness run won even on the single harness's
home turf, 48.5 against 46.0, while nearly tripling its Codex score [@yu2026openforgerl]. Harness
variety behaved like a regularizer, not a tax.

### What is missing

Not the idea, and not the tooling. Agent Lightning takes Qwen3.5-9B from 41.8 to 56.4 percent on
SWE-bench Verified using roughly 6,000 training examples and modest compute
[@he2026agentlightning]. That is not a frontier-scale result and is not meant to be. It shows the
work now fits inside what a small team, or one person with a rented GPU, can actually run.

What is missing is an open one. Every published result that trains across many harnesses comes from
a closed or semi-closed lab. Nobody has swept how many harnesses you need, or along which axes,
before the returns flatten out. The pieces all exist, in OpenEnv, in verifiers, in SkyRL, in Harbor.
The run does not.

That is what the rest of this article is: what a harness is, what an RL environment is, how to
capture trainable tokens out of an agent you do not control, and then the thing itself. A small open
model trained inside real agent harnesses and evaluated under four of them, Claude Code, Codex,
OpenCode and Mini-SWE-Agent, with the parts that did not work left in.
