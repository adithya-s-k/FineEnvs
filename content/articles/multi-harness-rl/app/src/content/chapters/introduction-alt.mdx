## Introduction

Read the release notes of the newest open-weight models and the same sentence keeps appearing in
different handwriting.

GLM-4.7 promises "significant improvements on complex tasks in mainstream agent frameworks such as
Claude Code, Kilo Code, Cline, and Roo Code" [@zai2025glm47]. Kimi K2 reports Terminal-Bench twice,
25.0 under Terminus and 30.0 inside Moonshot's own framework, as two rows of one table
[@kimi2025k2]. DeepSeek-V3.2 runs a robustness check across Claude Code and RooCode, then admits
its thinking-mode context management is incompatible with Terminus, so that number had to be
produced somewhere else entirely [@deepseek2025v32]. MiniMax M2 names a different harness for
almost every benchmark it lists [@minimax2025m2card].

Four labs, four ways of saying one thing: a score belongs to a model *and* a harness. Never to the
model alone. Which harness a model is good in has become a shipping claim, printed next to the
benchmark numbers, because users do not run weights. They run Claude Code, or Cline, or Codex, with
your weights behind it.

### Frontier labs stopped just reporting this and started training for it

poolside's Laguna report has a section titled Multi-harness Training, and its opening line is the
thesis of this article written by someone else first [@poolside2026laguna]:

> "To encourage generalization across diverse agent harnesses, our training data includes
> trajectories from external frameworks such as OpenHands, OpenCode, and Mini-SWE-Agent."

That is 1.3 billion tokens of agentic trajectories collected across three harnesses that are not
theirs, with the native behaviour of each deliberately left intact, "including custom subagent
spawning, context compaction, planning scaffolds, and reminder systems," so the model sees a broad
distribution of interaction patterns rather than one house style. Their RL phase then runs through
the harness they ship in production, so that "any change to the deployed harness is also a change
the policy is trained against."

The KwaiKAT team gives the failure a name, harness scaling, and the sharpest description of what
goes wrong without it [@kwaikat2026katcoder]:

> "In agentic RL, if training relies solely on a single fixed harness, the model often learns not
> 'how to solve the task' but 'how to solve the task under that particular harness's interface
> conventions.'"

The cost of learning the wrong one of those two things has been measured, and it is not a gentle
slope. Orchard trained a software-engineering agent in one harness and then ran it in others: 62.4
percent of SWE-bench Verified at home, 3.6 percent in a harness it had never seen. Training the
same model across harnesses instead gave 45.0 in that same foreign harness [@peng2026orchard]. Not
a worse score, a broken one, and their conclusion is flat: "multi-harness training is necessary."

The natural worry is that spreading training across harnesses buys robustness by giving up peak
performance on the one you care about. The only controlled ablation published so far says the
opposite: OpenForgeRL trained one base model on a single harness and on three, and the three-harness
run won even on the single harness's own turf, 48.5 against 46.0 [@yu2026openforgerl]. Harness
diversity behaved like a regularizer, not a tax.

### So what is missing

Not the idea, and not the tooling. Microsoft's Agent Lightning takes Qwen3.5-9B from 41.8 to 56.4
percent on SWE-bench Verified using 6,000 training examples and modest compute, which puts this
firmly inside what a small team can run [@he2026agentlightning].

What is missing is an open one. Every published result that actually trains across the harness
cross-product comes from a closed or semi-closed lab. Nobody has swept how many harnesses you
need, or along which axes, before the returns flatten out.

That is what the rest of this article is: what a harness is, what an RL environment is, how to
capture trainable tokens out of an agent you do not control, and then a small open model trained
across many harnesses, evaluated in all of them, with the parts that did not work left in.
